Groundwater in Kalaeloa and Honouliuli: What We Know

The H-POWER fuel spill has raised urgent questions about groundwater beneath West Honouliuli and its connections to the surrounding land and coastal waters. For generations, local communities have cared for these resources, and decades of scientific research and monitoring have produced valuable information about the area's groundwater. However, much of this information is scattered across reports and datasets that are difficult to find and interpret.

This page aims to bring together reports, datasets, scientific papers, and other evidence that can help answer important questions about groundwater and the potential movement of spilled fuel. Most importantly, this is a community-driven effort to crowdsource that information. We invite community members, researchers, agencies, and anyone with relevant knowledge or data to contribute their piece of the puzzle. By sharing resources and highlighting their key findings and limitations, we hope to build a clearer picture of what we know, what remains uncertain, and what we still need to learn.

This space is very much under construction and is in progress and is intended to be updated as new data and information is received and curated.

Help Us Put the Pieces Together

This project is intended to create a space for people to share what they know, and want others to understand about this area. Valuable information about West Honouliuli's groundwater may be sitting in old reports, monitoring datasets, scientific papers, or community records. We want to bring that knowledge together here.

Have something to contribute? Please send it to cshuler@hawaii.edu. If possible, include:

  • The report, dataset, paper, or other evidence (or a link to it).
  • 1–3 key findings, with page numbers where applicable.
  • Any important limitations or assumptions.

We will review contributions and add relevant findings to this growing community resource.

Question 1

Is the upper limestone aquifer highly permeable?

Yes. Multiple studies, including pumping tests at H-POWER itself, show that the upper limestone aquifer, where the fuel leaked, has high hydraulic conductivity, which is a measure of how fast fluids can move through the rock.

1. H-POWER groundwater pumping tests

Element Environmental (2020) — View report Appendix B, pp. 2–3

Findings

  • Pumping tests from wells located at H-POWER measured high hydraulic conductivity in the upper limestone.
  • A low-permeability sediment layer separates the upper and lower limestone aquifers.

Limitations

  • Results represent conditions near the tested wells, not necessarily the entire aquifer.
  • High hydraulic conductivity does not directly establish groundwater velocity OR direction which is influenced by pumping and injection

2. USGS ʻEwa groundwater investigation pp. 11–12 (hydraulic conductivity estimates) and pp. 37–39 (groundwater flow patterns)

Oki et al. (1996) — View report

Findings

  • Historical aquifer tests and tidal analyses indicate that the upper limestone is highly permeable, although hydraulic conductivity varies considerably across the area and between studies.

Limitations

  • Estimates vary substantially by location and method.
  • These historical measurements do not establish current groundwater velocities at H-POWER.

3. Campbell Industrial Park groundwater pumping tests

HECO (2006) — View Final EIS, pp. 4-54–4-56.

Findings

  • Pumping tests at nearby HECO and AES wells showed that more than 3,000 gallons of groundwater could be pumped every minute without substantially lowering the water table (less than 3 feet).
  • These results demonstrate that the limestone aquifer can supply large volumes of water with relatively little resistance to groundwater flow.

Question 2

Is groundwater hydraulically connected to the ocean?

Yes. Groundwater monitoring across the ʻEwa Plain shows that water levels rise and fall in response to ocean tides, even thousands of feet inland. This demonstrates a strong hydraulic connection between the ocean and the coastal limestone aquifer.

1. ʻEwa Plain groundwater tidal monitoring

HECO (2006) — View Final EIS, pp. 4-56 and 4-59 (Figure 4.8).

Tom Nance (2013) — View Hoakalei EIS (2014), pp. 4-39–4-40 (Figure 4.23).

Findings

  • Groundwater at Campbell Industrial Park responds strongly to ocean tides, with some wells showing fluctuations approximately half the size of ocean tides.
  • At Hoakalei, more to the east tidal fluctuations were observed in groundwater wells as far as 3,590 feet inland.

Limitations

  • These measurements demonstrate hydraulic connectivity but do not establish groundwater or contaminant travel speed.
  • Measurements were collected at different locations and times, somr far from H-power, but some close.

2. ʻEwa caprock water-level investigation

Bauer (1996) — View CWRM Report, pp. 19–20 and Figure 19 (p. 54).

Findings

  • Groundwater levels in the Puʻuloa area can fluctuate by approximately 6 inches daily in response to ocean tides.
  • Groundwater-level fluctuations in general are closely related to changes in ocean water levels.

Limitations

  • Measurements were collected east of H-POWER.
  • Tidal responses do not establish groundwater flow direction or velocity.

3. Kīpuka o Kāhinahina sinkhole monitoring

Tagura & Shuler — UH Mānoa SOEST Research Poster — Ongoing Exploration of the Hydrogeology of Kīpuka o Kāhinahina, p. 1 (Figure 7).

Findings

  • Water-level, temperature, and salinity loggers have been installed in the Kalua sinkholes to characterize groundwater conditions.
  • Preliminary comparisons with ocean tides indicate that groundwater levels respond to tidal fluctuations approximately 1,050 feet inland, providing new evidence of hydraulic connectivity at the Kīpuka itself.

Limitations

  • Monitoring and analysis are ongoing; quantitative tidal-response results have not yet been formally published.
  • Tidal fluctuations demonstrate hydraulic connectivity but do not establish how quickly groundwater or fuel moves through the aquifer.

Question 3

How fast could the fuel migrate through the aquifer?

We don't know yet. Although the upper limestone aquifer is highly permeable, groundwater movement also depends on the hydraulic gradient. Extensive industrial groundwater pumping and injection in the area can alter this gradient, potentially changing both the speed and direction of groundwater flow. Previous studies on the ʻEwa Plain provide estimates of groundwater travel time, but none specifically for the H-POWER fuel spill.

1. Honouliuli groundwater travel-time study

Lau et al. (1989) — View original report — UH Mānoa ScholarSpace p. 61 (travel-time calculation), p. 62 (Figure 37, location map).

Findings

  • An older report from Ewa beach area estimated that groundwater could travel approximately 3,500 feet in 308 days through the limestone aquifer near the Honouliuli Wastewater Treatment Plant.
  • That corresponds to an estimated groundwater movement rate of approximately 11.4 feet per day. However, fuel can behave differently from groundwater, so this rate should not be interpreted as the speed at which spilled fuel would travel.

Limitations

  • Travel time was calculated using measured groundwater gradients but assumed hydraulic conductivity and porosity.
  • The study was conducted several miles from H-POWER, and the suspected nitrate source was not confirmed. These results do not directly predict how quickly fuel will migrate at H-POWER.

Question 4

Where will the fuel go? Do we know groundwater flow direction at H-POWER?

Not yet. And this will be very hard to calculate. Groundwater generally moves from areas of higher to lower water levels, but extensive industrial pumping, injection, and ocean tides can alter these patterns. Determining where the fuel may travel requires measurements of current groundwater recharge and pumping, and can be very complicated when tides influence the aquifer requiring variable-density groundwater modeling. (Very complex and requires extensive data)

1. Industrial pumping and groundwater flow near H-POWER

CWRM (2008) — View Staff Submittal, pp. 4–5.

Cameron (2022) — View UH Mānoa Thesis, pp. 34–36 (Figure 23, p. 35).

Findings

  • Multiple sources indicate Industrial facilities are pumping millions of gallons of groundwater daily, from many wells nearby the spill location including approximately 11 million gallons per day from AES wells just 500 feet south of H-POWER, and from supply wells at H-POWER.
  • Groundwater modeling identified areas around Campbell Industrial Park where pumping lowered groundwater levels to near or below sea level, potentially altering groundwater flow directions.

Limitations

  • Industrial pumping is variable and requires gathering data from multiple sources. Missing any of the pumping can throw off calculations regarding groundwater flow directions
  • The available groundwater models have uncertainties and does not establish current flow directions at H-POWER.

2. Current H-POWER fuel spill investigation

City & County of Honolulu (2026) — View investigation updates, Questions 10 and 14.

Findings

  • Monitoring indicates that fuel contamination has extended beyond H-POWER's property boundaries to the west and south.
  • The apparent spread of fuel in multiple directions, rather than simply toward the nearest coastline, suggests that local groundwater gradients or other subsurface pathways may be influencing its movement.

Limitations

  • The distribution of fuel does not necessarily establish groundwater flow direction or where contamination will travel next.
  • Publicly available monitoring results do not yet provide the groundwater elevations and flow directions needed to predict future migration.
